A Beginner's Guide to Losing Body Fat

Fitness information can be very overwhelming when adjusting to a new diet, moving your body more, and going through everyday life all at the same time. This Beginner's Guide can help get you moving in the right direction while still allowing room for you to choose what works best for your needs.

Introduction

Losing body fat can seem complicated, especially when there is so much information about diets, calories, workouts, and what you should or should not be doing. In reality, there is no single approach that works perfectly for everyone. This Beginner's Guide brings together the basics of fat loss in one place and breaks them down into manageabe steps. From understanding what body fat is to making better food decisions and moving more, this guide will help you build a foundation that can be adjusted to fit your own goals and lifetsyle.

Purpose

The purpose of creating this Beginner's Guide is to make the process of losing body fat feel less overwhelming and more approachable. With so much conflicting information available, it can be difficult to know where to begin or what advice to follow. This guide breaks the process down into simple steps by helping you understand body fat, determine your starting point, gain a better understanding of nutrition, and become more active. Rather than presenting one strict approach, this guide provides reliable information you can apply and adjust to fit your individual lifestyle and goals.

Audience

This guide is intended for beginners who want to lose body fat but may not know where or how to start. iif you are unfamiliar with concepts such as body composition, calorie intake, nutrition, and different forms of exercise then you are in the right place. You may also be balancing work, school, family, or other responsibilities, so I've provided information that is realistic and easy to understand. This guide is designed to give these you a foundation for making informed choices without expecting you to completely change your lifestyle all at once.

Step One

Understanding What Body Fat Is

Body fat, also known as adipose tissue is a normal and necessary part of our bodies. Although it may feel like the goal is to lose as much as possible, body fat is actually essential for our bodies to function as they should. According to the Cleveland Clinic it stores energy, protects your organs, and helps insulate your body. In other words, body fat itself isn’t the problem. The goal of fat loss is to reduce the amount of excess body fat while still maintaining a healthy amount that allows your body to function normally.

Understanding what happens to stored fat and how it is lost will make the process a little less confusing. Your body needs energy every day, whether you’re working out, walking around at your job, or simply sitting still. Some of that energy comes from the foods you eat, but your body can also use energy that’s already stored. When you consistently consume fewer calories than you expend, your body begins using stored energy, including fat. This is why calorie deficits, protein prioritization, and going to the gym are such popular topics when it comes to losing body fat.

That being said, fat doesn’t simply burn off. The Healthy Life's video on how fat leaves our bodies explains the actual process. Much of the fat leaves your body as carbon dioxide when you breathe, while the rest leaves as water when you sweat. When this is considered alongside the Cleaveland Clinic's explanation of fat as stored energy, fat loss becomes a much simpler concept: your body stores excess energy as fat and can use that stored energy when you are consuming less than you’re using. Before deciding how much fat you want to lose, though, you first need to know what your starting point is.

Key Takeaway

The goal isn't to eliminate body fat, but to understand it and work toward a healthy body composition.

02

Step Two

Knowing Where You Stand

Before making changes, it helps to understand your starting point. Many people commit to calorie deficits and methods they see other people using, but the truth is that losing body fat is a lot more individual. Your height, weight, activity level, and daily habits all play a role in deciding which approach works for you. This is why figuring out where you stand should come before deciding how much to eat or how much weight you want to lose.

One measurement you’ve probably heard of is BMI (Body Mass Index). The American Heart Association says understanding your BMI is an important part of being healthy. It is easy to calculate, which makes it a useful place to start, but it doesn’t directly measure your body fat percentage. Two people can have the same BMI while having different amounts of muscle and body fat. This doesn’t make BMI useless; it just means the number needs context. The same idea applies to TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure). A TDEE Calculator estimates how many calories your body uses throughout an entire day. This estimate can be especially helpful when trying to figure out your calorie deficit. If you know how much energy your body uses, it’s easier to find out how much you should eat rather than choosing a random calorie goal that worked for someone else.

Both BMI and TDEE have their limitations. A calculator doesn’t know your exact movement throughout the day or exactly how many calories your body burns. This is where other measurements, such as DEXA scans, come in handy because they can provide a more detailed look into your body composition. Think of BMI, TDEE, and body composition measurements as tools. BMI provides a height-to-weight ratio, TDEE estimates your daily energy needs, and body composition measurements provide insight into what your weight is actually made up of. Knowing where you stand isn’t about finding one perfect measurement. It’s about collecting enough information about your body to be able to make informed decisions and adjustments based on its needs.

Key Takeaway

Use BMI and TDEE as guides, not exact measurements. Your individual needs and progress matter more than a single number.

03

Step Three

Eat Better

Nutrition plays a very important role in losing body fat. At its simplest, losing fat requires a calorie deficit, meaning you’re consume less calories than your body uses. That being said, knowing that you need a calorie deficit and actually sticking to one are two different things. When first starting a calorie deficit, people usually experience intense cravings or feel like they’re constantly thinking about food. This is where what you eat becomes just as important as knowing how much you eat.

In her podcast about overeating, Mandy Meyer explains that eating less does not have to mean skipping meals or constantly feeling hungry. Instead, choosing filling foods, paying attention to portions, and understanding your eating habits can make it easier to maintain a calorie deficit. Bodybuilder Krunoslav builds on this idea by emphasizing food quality rather than focusing only on calorie numbers. Together, these sources show that calorie deficit may result in weight loss, but your food choices and ability to maintain your deficit are ultimately the deciding factors.

Have you ever heard the saying “quality over quantity”? Well that applies to calories too. Imagine two people both consuming 2,000 calories daily while in a calorie deficit. One gets most of their calories from highly processed snacks, while the other eats mostly whole foods such as fruits, veggies, and protein. Both could still lose weight because they’re in a calorie deficit, but they may have completely different experiences. The main difference will be in how each of them feels throughout the process. Whole foods contain macronutrients that support brain function and hormones, making a diet easier to stick to.

Eating better doesn’t mean you have to eat perfectly or stop enjoying the foods you love. Meyer’s focus on sustainable eating habits and Krunoslav’s focus on food quality point towards the same bigger idea: the best approach is one you can actually maintain.

Key Takeaway

Focus on eating habits you can maintain rather than following a strict or temporary diet.

04

Step Four

Move More

Being more active can support fat loss by increasing the amount of energy your body uses, but that doesn’t mean you have to spend hours in the gym every day after work. Jeremy Ethier's fat-loss video explains how both exercise and everyday movement contribute to the amount of calories you burn each day. Research published in Frontiers in Physiology adds another piece to this by taking a look into how exercise can influence fat loss and where fat is stored throughout our bodies. Together, these sources show that movement isn’t just about burning as many calories as possible during one workout. It is about your overall activity and the types of exercises you do.

So what exercises should you do? Any.

Whether you choose strength training, cardio, or another form of exercise you enjoy that is sustainable, moving your body is the most important. You don’t have to choose between lifting weights and cardio or assume that one is the “best” way to lose fat. It is okay to skip leg day every now and then and each gym session doesn’t have to be accompanied by the stairmaster. As long as you are consistent with whichever route you choose, you are likely to see improvements in your body composition.

More importantly, moving more should fit into your actual life. You can have the “perfect” workout plan on paper, but it will not do much for you if you hate it and stop after two weeks. Walking more, signing up for a local intramural sports league, lifting weights, or finding another activity you enjoy can all be ways to become more active. Both sources point back to the same idea seen in Step 03 Eat Better: consistency matters more than perfection.

Key Takeaway

Start with realistic ways to move more and gradually build an exercise routine you can maintain.
